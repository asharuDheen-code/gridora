Battery Lifecycle Management

Capacity fade is predictable if you measure the right things. How state of health is tracked over a twenty-year asset life, and what happens at the end of it.

Capacity fade is predictable if you measure the right things. How state of health is tracked over a twenty-year asset life, and what happens at the end of it.

What actually ages a battery

Two processes run in parallel and it helps to keep them separate.

Calendar ageing happens whether or not the system is used. It is driven mostly by temperature and by the state of charge the pack sits at while idle. A pack parked at full charge in a hot enclosure ages measurably faster than the same pack held part-charged and cool.

Cycle ageing is driven by throughput and by depth of discharge. Shallow cycles are disproportionately cheap: the damage per kilowatt-hour moved rises sharply as cycles get deeper.

Which one dominates depends on the duty. A demand-management system that cycles lightly a few times a week is usually calendar-limited. A daily-shifting utility asset is cycle-limited.

Design-stage decision

Augmentation strategy is the item most often deferred and most expensive to retrofit. Decide it before the layout is fixed, not after.

Measuring state of health

State of health is an estimate, and it is only as good as the conditions it is measured under. A capacity figure taken at a different temperature, rate or state of charge than the last one is not comparable to it.

What makes the trend usable is a repeatable reference test: the same rate, the same temperature band, the same depth, run on a schedule. Once you have three or four of those, the slope tells you far more than any single measurement.

Worth watching alongside capacity: internal resistance, which usually rises before capacity falls, and cell-to-cell divergence, which is often the earliest signal that something specific is going wrong rather than everything ageing together.

Operating inside the envelope

Warranties are written as an envelope — cycles per year, depth of discharge, temperature range, minimum and maximum state of charge. Operating outside it does not necessarily break anything, but it does change the conversation when a claim is made.

The practical approach is to enforce the envelope in the energy management system rather than rely on operator discipline. If the dispatch algorithm cannot physically schedule a cycle that breaches the warranty, nobody has to remember not to.

Planning augmentation

A system sized exactly to requirement on day one is undersized by year five. There are three ways to handle that, and the choice should be made at design stage because it affects the layout:

  • Oversize at the start. Simple, but you pay for capacity you cannot use for years.
  • Leave space for augmentation. Add racks or blocks later. Cheapest over the life, provided the space, the conversion headroom and the cable capacity were left there.
  • Accept the fade. Viable where the duty is power-limited rather than energy-limited.

Mixing new and aged racks on the same bus needs care — the management system has to be able to handle a fleet with different histories, and not every one can.

End of life

End of life for a stationary application is a threshold, not a failure. It is usually defined as the point where remaining capacity no longer meets the duty, which for most projects lands somewhere between 60 and 70 per cent of original.

What happens next is a commercial and regulatory question as much as a technical one, and it is worth having an answer to it in the contract rather than discovering it in year fifteen. The practical items are: who owns the modules at end of life, who pays for removal, and what the return or recycling route is.
