Case Study: 10 MW Solar Storage Project

A worked example of how a 10 MW solar plant with 20 MWh of storage is sized, sequenced and commissioned — using demonstration figures throughout.

A worked example of how a 10 MW solar plant with 20 MWh of storage is sized, sequenced and commissioned — using demonstration figures throughout.

The site

A 10 MW ground-mount solar plant with an existing export connection, built with a DC-to-AC ratio high enough that clear days clipped output for two to three hours around noon. The owner wanted to recover the clipped energy and to shift some production into the evening window where the tariff was better.

About this case study

An illustrative worked example. The site, the figures and the sequence are representative of how a project like this is approached, not a record of a completed installation.

Sizing the system

Sizing started with a year of five-minute generation data rather than with a target capacity. Two numbers came out of it: how much energy was being lost to clipping on a typical clear day, and how much energy the evening window could absorb at the better tariff.

The clipped energy set the floor — there is no point installing more storage than the plant can spare. The evening window set the ceiling. In this case they converged on 20 MWh of usable energy at 5 MW, giving four hours at rated power.

DC or AC coupling

DC coupling recovers clipped energy that never reaches the inverters, and avoids one conversion step. It also shares the existing inverter capacity, which constrains how fast the storage can discharge.

AC coupling is independent of the array and simpler to retrofit, but it cannot see clipped energy at all — by the time energy reaches the AC side, the clipping has already happened.

Because recovering clipped energy was the primary objective here, the majority went DC-coupled, with a smaller AC-coupled block to give the plant controller independent discharge capability in the evening.

Commissioning sequence

The plant was already generating and the owner did not want to stop it, so the work was sequenced around production:

  1. Civil works, cable routes and the plinth, with no interruption to generation.
  2. Blocks delivered, positioned and mechanically installed.
  3. DC connections made string by string during a scheduled low-irradiance window.
  4. Controls integrated and dry-tested against the plant controller before energisation.
  5. Staged energisation, one block at a time, with a witnessed performance test at the end of each stage.

What we would do differently

Two things. The communications integration between the storage controller and the existing plant controller took longer than the electrical work, largely because the protocol mapping was agreed late — that should have been settled at design stage rather than on site.

Second, the ambient design case should have been set from measured site data rather than regional averages. It was fine in the end, but the margin was thinner than it needed to be.
